**"The Gates of Morning"**

**CHAPTER I: THE CANOE BUILDER**

Dick stood poised on a coral outcrop, his gaze sweeping south across the vast, tranquil expanse of Karolin's lagoon. Behind him, the outer sea roared, its breakers crashing relentlessly, sending a fine mist scattering on the wind. Before him, the lagoon lay calm as a boundless lake, an endless blue, dotted by the graceful arcs of fishing gulls. And this serene, forty-mile circle of water—a sea unto itself, at times a tempest, at others a placid azure pond—was now his. He had claimed it just yesterday.

The women, children, and young men, busy along the sun-drenched beach, mending nets, playing their spirited games, or tending to the taro patches – they were all his people. His were the canoes pulled high onto the sand, and his the empty shelters where formidable war canoes once rested on their rollers. But as his eyes drifted from the lagoon to those vacant houses, his brow furrowed. He turned his back on the inner peace, facing the thundering breakers of the outer beach and the northern sea. Out there, beyond the visible horizon, lay Palm Tree Island, a place of stunning beauty, yet teeming with menace.

Little Tari, the son of Le Taioi the net-maker, sat nearby on the warm coral, looking up at him. Tari understood little of the world, but he knew that Karolin's men had been swept away by war, leaving the women, boys, and children like himself vulnerable, without a leader. Then, just yesterday, from the northern sea, a strange new figure had arrived in an unfamiliar boat, accompanied by Katafa, the girl who had been lost at sea years ago while fishing. He was, the women whispered, a chief sent by the gods themselves. Unconcerned with the identities of these gods, and now out of earshot of his mother, Tari, with a child’s simple honesty, asked directly:

“Taori,” little Tari piped, “who are you?” ( _é kamina tai_ )

Could Dick have truly answered, would the child have grasped the strange, complex tale? "Tari, my past stretches beyond your comprehension. My name was once Dick Lestrange, and when I was smaller than you, I was left alone with Kearney, the gruff sailor who raised me on the island you call Marua (Palm Tree), fifty miles north, unseen from here. We lived there; I grew from a boy into a man. Kearney taught me to fish, to spear, and he crafted toys for me – small ships, unlike the canoes of these islands. Then, Tari, long ago, Katafa arrived, swept to Marua in a storm from this very place. She lived with us until Kearney died, leaving just the two of us. She taught me her language, the tongue of Karolin, and named me Taori. We loved each other, and might have lived forever on Marua, had not a large vessel appeared, filled with brutal men from the eastern islands of Melanesia. They came to exploit the land, then turned on the white men who brought them, killing them and burning the ship. We escaped in our small boat, taking everything we cherished, even Kearney's little ships, steering towards Karolin, guided by the lagoon's glimmering light in the night sky."

But the words caught in his throat. His own name, "Dick," felt like a distant echo, swallowed by the tropic sun and the vast ocean. Kearney, the sailor who had been his world, was all but a blurred memory, lost in the luminous haze of his past. For those long shipwrecked and isolated, the past becomes a fog; for Dick, it truly began with Katafa's arrival on Marua. Everything before that seemed consumed by the intense tropic light that bathed the island and sea, the fierce storms that swept the coconut groves, the mists of the rainy seasons. Kearney might have been entirely forgotten, were it not for the small ships he had carved as playthings for the boy who was now a man.

He looked down at the questioning child. “I am Taori, Tari tatu. Why do you ask?”

“I don’t know,” the child replied simply. “I just ask, like I breathe. But the grown-ups – _madyana_ – never answer Tari’s questions. Ai, the fish!” His agile mind had already darted away, drawn by the excited shouts of children hauling in a net. He rose and trotted off.

Dick turned his gaze north again. The child’s question had stirred his mind, and he saw once more the schooner that had anchored at Palm Tree, only to be consumed by fire at the hands of the Melanesians. He relived his and Katafa’s desperate escape in the old dinghy Kearney had taught him to navigate as a boy. He saw their landing on this very beach yesterday, the women and children swarming around him, the man they believed sent by the gods to be their chief and leader.

As he stared north, the memory of the men from whom he and Katafa had fled tainted the beauty of the sea and sky. There was no immediate threat from the men who had seized Palm Tree; they had no canoes. But they *would* build them. And just as he had, they would surely see the distant, shimmering glow of Karolin’s lagoon reflected in the sky, and they would come. It might be a long time, but they would come.

Dick was a man shaped by two worlds – island life and a dimly remembered heritage that sparked foresight and strategic thought. That was why, that very morning, he had dispatched a canoe to the southern beach to fetch Aioma, Palia, and Tafata – three elders, too old for war, yet masterful canoe-builders. That was why, surveying his gathered tribe, his eyes had sharpened to the fact that nearly a hundred youths were ripening into fighting age. But beneath all these calculations, igniting and animating his mind, elevating his courage to soaring heights, lay his profound passion for Katafa, his other self, dearer to him than his own life, now vaguely, yet undeniably, threatened.

War canoes! Did he intend to confront any invaders within the lagoon, or as they approached the shore? Or did he vaguely plan to become the aggressor, crushing the danger at its source before it could fully develop? Who could say?

A soft touch on his shoulder. He turned to face Katafa. A strand of her dark hair, escaping its elastic vine, danced back on the breeze like a wild bird’s feather. Her luminous, dark eyes, instead of meeting his, were fixed on the very point he had been staring at – the due-north sea line.

“Look!” said Katafa.

At long intervals, and under specific weather conditions, Palm Tree Island, though far beyond the horizon, became visible from Karolin through a trick of the light – a mirage. They had seen it last evening, and now, once again, it was beginning to materialize, to bloom into being, a mysterious smudge low in the southern sky, a dull spot in the dazzling sea-haze, that gradually deepened and solidified until, as if sketched by an unseen hand, the island appeared, beautiful as a dream, ethereal yet vivid.

With her hand resting on his shoulder, they stood in silence, their minds unburdened by scientific understanding, knowing nothing of mirage. Their eyes were fixed on the place from which they had escaped, now rising so strangely beyond the distant sea line, as if to confront them. They saw again the horde of figures on the beach, monstrous as nightmare forms, and felt anew the wind that swelled their dinghy's sail as it raced for safety and the open sea. Again, they heard the savage yells of the Melanesians, wild with rum plundered from the schooner they had brought in and then burned. And there, before them, lay the scene of their tragedy – a beautiful image that revealed nothing of the demons that still inhabited it.

Then, as Dick gazed upon this loveliness, which was both a threat and a warning, his nostrils flared, and his eyes darkened with a raw, primal hatred. They had threatened him – that was trivial. They had threatened Katafa – that was everything. And they still threatened her.

Someday, they would come. The vision of Palm Tree seemed to echo what instinct already told him. They would build canoes, and seeing the lagoon's mirror-light in the sky, they would come. Those men had no women, and here were women. Instinct subtly whispered that just as he had been drawn to Katafa, so too would these men be drawn to the women of Karolin. They would scour the horizon for an island whose tribe they could raid for its women, and seeing the lagoon light, they would come.

Ah, if only he had truly known, danger lurked not just to the north, but wherever greed, desire, or hatred roamed across that azure sea – not only among those perceived as savages, but also among the 'wolves' of so-called civilization. For Dick, the world was this ring of water, these two islands; there was no Europe, no America, no history beyond his and Katafa’s brief lives. And yet, even in that short life, he had learned to dread men, and had glimpsed the foundational truth of all history – humanity's instinct for war, rapine, and destruction.

Then, gradually, the vision of Palm Tree began to fade, finally vanishing like a snuffed-out flame. As they turned from the sea back to the lagoon, Katafa pointed across the water to a canoe approaching from the southern beach. It was the canoe Dick had sent for the builders, and leaving the coral, they descended to the white sand of the inner beach to meet it.
